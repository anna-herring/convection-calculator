1. Scope and operating point

Xenon dissolving into de-ionised water in a porous core, isothermal at ~40 °C, 1–20 MPa (see the hydrate constraint in §3 for why 40 °C). The dashboard estimates (i) the Rayleigh–Darcy number governing convective onset and (ii) the X-ray attenuation of the Xe-saturated solution relative to pure water.

Xe is a dense supercritical fluid at these conditions. Tc = 289.733 K = 16.58 °C and Pc = 5.842 MPa, so at 40 °C the reduced temperature is Tr = 1.081 and the reduced pressure spans Pr = 0.17–3.42. The Xe phase is always supercritical (T > Tc at any operating temperature here) and becomes a dense fluid across the range (ρ ≈ 53–1972 kg/m³), not an ideal gas. This is why a reference-quality equation of state is used rather than a cubic one (§2).

Sign convention. Dissolved Xe makes water denser (V̅m = 46.5 cm³/mol carries M = 131.29 g/mol), so the saturated layer sinks and the instability propagates downward from the Xe/water interface.

2. Gas-phase equation of state and fugacity

Xe properties come from the Helmholtz-energy formulation of Lemmon & Span (2006) [1] as implemented in CoolProp [2]. The residual Helmholtz energy αr(τ,δ) gives the compressibility and fugacity coefficient directly:

Z = 1 + δ (∂αr/∂δ)τ

ln φ = αr + (Z − 1) − ln Z δ = ρ/ρc reduced density, τ = Tc/T inverse reduced temperature, φ fugacity coefficient, f = φP fugacity.

Why not Peng–Robinson?

Peng–Robinson [3] is retained only as a fallback if a state point cannot be evaluated. Cubic EoS are least accurate near the critical point, and that is not far off where this experiment sits. Measured against the reference EoS at 40 °C:

Because xsat ∝ φ (§3), that 1.5–4.3 % bias propagates linearly into Δρ, Ra and the X-ray contrast. When this switch was made (then at 20 °C / 10 MPa) it raised both Ra and xsat by 3.4 %.

3. Aqueous solubility

Henry's law with the Krichevsky–Kasarnovsky pressure correction [4], driven by the fugacity from §2:

xsat = fXe(P,T) / K(P,T)

K(P,T) = H(T) · exp[ V̅m (P − Pv) / RT ]

H(T) = H298 / exp[ −ΔsolnH/R · (1/T − 1/298.15) ] xsat mole fraction Xe at saturation; H Henry constant [MPa]; V̅m partial molar volume of dissolved Xe; Pv water vapour pressure; ΔsolnH/R = 2300 K (van’t Hoff temperature coefficient — verified; Fernández-Prini et al. 2003 [20] gives 2200 K, published range 2200–2500 K, which spans 4.7 % in xsat at 40 °C, 1.7 % at 20 °C — the spread grows with distance from the 298.15 K reference).

H298 is converted from the Henry solubility constant Hcp = 4.3×10−5 mol/(m³·Pa) at 298.15 K — the value recommended by Abraham & Matteoli (1988) [18] and Clever (1979b) [19], as compiled by Sander (2015) [5]. Converting to the mol/(kg·bar) form used internally is a factor of 100, since 55510 mol/m³ × 0.018015 kg/mol = 1000. An earlier version applied ×10, making Xe solubility — and hence Δρ, Ra and the X-ray contrast — a factor of 10 too low. The same slip affected Kr and H₂ in the sibling tool. validate_solubility() now checks the model against Sander’s value in its native units, so the conversion itself is covered by the test.

The Henry constant actually evaluated is Eq. (15) of Rasoolzadeh et al. [22], a published correlation rather than a two-parameter fit:

ln(HXe,water/bar) = 39.273 z − 36.855 z² + ln(1.01325),   z = 1 − 188.78/T Valid across the full temperature range of the hydrate data. It agrees with the compiled Hcp above to 1.9 % at 25 °C — an independent cross-check on the corrected constant. The van’t Hoff form drifts ~10 % by 5 °C and ~5 % by 40 °C, so it is retained only for comparison (_henry_MPa_vanthoff).

Clathrate hydrate: the limit of validity

Xenon forms an sI clathrate hydrate (Xe·5.90H2O). Above the three-phase equilibrium pressure a solid hydrate is thermodynamically stable and the dissolution model here does not describe the system — there is a third phase. Fitting a Henry/Krichevsky–Kasarnovsky form to high-pressure solubility data taken inside the hydrate field pushes the apparent V̅m to unphysical values (~125 cm³/mol against a true ~42–52 cm³/mol), because the fitted parameter absorbs the hydrate contribution. Rasoolzadeh et al. use vXe∞ = 46 cm³/mol in the same Krichevsky–Kasarnovsky framework, independently supporting the 46.5 used here, and note that it may be treated as pressure-independent below the solvent critical temperature.

The boundary is taken from the measured Lw–H–V three-phase data of Rasoolzadeh et al. (2020) [22] — all 87 points of their Table 4, covering 273.15–343.75 K and 0.153–376 MPa (tensimeter, Cailletet apparatus and high-pressure autoclave; u(T) 0.01–0.02 K, u(P) 0.025 kPa–0.05 MPa). The code interpolates ln Phyd linearly in T between these points; earlier data of Makogon et al. [21] are consistent over their narrower range.

Phyd [MPa]0.250.420.721.171.963.386.3721.953.5
At 20 °C the boundary is only ~1.17 MPa. Lowering pressure is therefore a weak lever — a 1–5 MPa span at 20 °C is ~95 % inside the hydrate field, which is why the default operating point is 40 °C rather than 20 °C. Temperature is the effective control, and the window opens very steeply: 3.4 MPa at 30 °C, 6.4 MPa at 35 °C, but 21.9 MPa at 40 °C. The curve has a pronounced knee near 310 K where the Xe-rich phase becomes dense, so no single Clausius–Clapeyron fit represents it — a two-point fit used here previously was 57 % low at 40 °C. The dashboard shades the stable region on every panel and reports the fraction of the swept range inside it.

V̅m enters twice, in the same direction. It raises the solution molar volume (lowering Δρ, §4) and it sits in the Krichevsky–Kasarnovsky exponent (lowering xsat). The two effects compound rather than cancel.

4. Solution density and buoyancy

Ideal molar-volume mixing between water and the partial molar volume of dissolved Xe:

Mmix = x MXe + (1−x) MH2O
Vmix = x V̅m + (1−x) MH2O/ρw

ρsat = Mmix / Vmix     Δρ = ρsat − ρw ρw DI-water density from IAPWS-95 [6] at the local T, P. Mass fraction of Xe wXe = x MXe/Mmix, and dissolved concentration CXe = wXe ρsat.

At 8 MPa and 40 °C, xsat = 2.51×10−3 gives CXe = 18.2 g/L (≡ kg/m³, 138 mM) and Δρ = 11.8 kg/m³ — comparable to CO2/brine systems, so the buoyancy forcing is substantial rather than marginal. Ra remains far more sensitive to k and H than to pressure, because xsat is close to saturated in P.

5. Water properties and diffusivity

Density from IAPWS-95 [6] and viscosity from the IAPWS 2008 formulation [7], both evaluated at the local T and P (not 1 atm — that distinction caused a spurious discontinuity in an earlier version when the saturation line was crossed).

Aqueous Xe diffusivity is scaled from 25 °C by the Stokes–Einstein relation, in which D η/T is invariant:

D(T) = D25 · (T / 298.15) · (ηw(298.15) / ηw(T)) D25 aqueous diffusivity at 25 °C; ηw water viscosity.

D25 = 1.47×10−9 m²/s for Xe in water, from the sparingly-soluble-gas measurements of Jähne, Heinz & Dietrich (1987) [8]. This sits correctly in the noble-gas series — diffusivity decreases monotonically with atomic size, so Xe must fall below Kr (1.84×10−9).

Ra is inversely proportional to D, so this is one of the most leveraged inputs in the model — more so than pressure across the whole 1–20 MPa range. For reference, the sensitivity (ROBU0 at 8 MPa, 40 °C):

6. Convective onset: Rayleigh–Darcy number

Ra = Δρ g k H / (μw φ D) k permeability [m²]; H core height [m]; φ porosity; g = 9.81 m/s².

Compared against the classical linear-stability threshold for a saturated porous layer, Horton & Rogers (1945) [9] and Lapwood (1948) [10]:

Rac = 4π² ≈ 39.5

The dashboard labels Ra > 4Rac “vigorous convection”, Ra > Rac “convective”, Ra > 0.5Rac “marginal”, else diffusion-dominated. Inverting Ra = Rac gives the core height at which a sample first convects, since Ra ∝ H.

7. Velocity, length and time scales

uD = k Δρ g / μw   (Darcy buoyancy flux)     upore = uD/φ

ℓ* = φD/uD     t* = φ²D/uD²

tconv = Hφ/uD = H/upore     tdiff = H²/D Darcy velocity is a flux per unit total area; the fluid itself moves faster by 1/φ, which is why tconv carries the φ.

The two timescales are not independent of Ra — their ratio is Ra:

tdiff/tconv = (H²/D) / (Hφ/uD) = H uD/(φD) = Ra

verified numerically to within floating-point error. The timescale card therefore restates panel (a) in clock time, which is the form needed to choose an acquisition cadence.

Onset of the instability is reported as ~10² t*, an order-of-magnitude scaling consistent with the linear-stability and simulation results of Riaz et al. (2006) [11] and Slim (2014) [12]. It is a scaling, not a prediction; the φ bookkeeping in t* differs between sources.

8. Permeability estimates (Kozeny–Carman)

All permeabilities in the presets are nominal grade values or estimates, not measurements on the specific cores. ROBU00 and ROBU0 have no quoted permeability and are extrapolated by Kozeny–Carman [13, 14]:

k = C · φ³ d² / (1 − φ)² d geometric mean of the quoted pore-size range; C a shape/tortuosity constant calibrated against a grade of known k.

Both are assigned ROBU1’s porosity (0.38), so the porosity group cancels exactly and KC reduces to k ∝ d²:

kROBU0 = 200 D × (200.0/126.5)² = 500 D
kROBU00 = 200 D × (353.6/126.5)² = 1563 D

Calibrating C on the known grades gives 0.0876 (ROBU1), 0.1109 (ROBU2), 0.1102 (ROBU3). ROBU2 and ROBU3 agree to 0.6 %, so the functional form describes the series well, but ROBU1 sits ~21 % below that trend. Anchoring on ROBU2/ROBU3 instead would scale both extrapolations up by ~1.26×. Since Ra ∝ k, that is a direct 26 % uncertainty in Ra for these two grades.

9. X-ray attenuation

Beer–Lambert with the mass-attenuation mixture rule, on NIST XCOM / Hubbell & Seltzer tabulations [15, 16] interpolated log–log in energy:

μw = ρw (μ/ρ)H2O
μsat = ρsat [ wXe(μ/ρ)Xe + (1−wXe)(μ/ρ)H2O ]

I/I0 = exp(−μL)     λ1/e = 1/μ μ linear attenuation coefficient [cm−1]; wXe mass fraction of dissolved Xe; L path length.

Panel (b) plots μsat/μw; panel (d) plots the equivalent 1/e length ratio, which is its exact reciprocal and is independent of path length:

λsat/λw = μw/μsat = (μsat/μw)−1

The Xe K-edge dominates the energy choice

Xe has a K absorption edge at 34.5614 keV, across which (μ/ρ)Xe jumps by a factor of 5.4 (6.129 → 33.16 cm²/g). Interpolation is performed separately below and above the edge so it is never smoothed across. Operating just above the edge (~35 keV) rather than below it (30 keV) changes μsat/μw from 1.43 to 2.86, and the radiographic contrast from 3.0 % to 11.0 % (at 8 MPa, 40 °C).

What the matrix and porosity do

For a difference measurement between Xe-free and Xe-saturated states in the same core, the solid matrix term is identical in both and cancels exactly from the change in optical depth:

Δ(μL) = [(1−φ)μs + φμsat]L − [(1−φ)μs + φμw]L = φ Δμfluid L

So the matrix sets the transmitted flux (and therefore the noise) but contributes nothing to the contrast. Two different numbers apply depending on the measurement:

  • Projection radiography — path-integrated, contrast = φ Δμ L (≈11 % for a 5 mm, φ = 0.38 core at 35 keV, 8 MPa, 40 °C).
  • Pore-resolved tomography — a reconstructed pore voxel is entirely fluid, so it swings the full μsat/μw ≈ 2.9× (panel b). Neither φ nor the matrix enters this contrast; they enter only through reconstruction noise.

11. Assumptions and limitations

  1. Tortuosity is neglected. Molecular D is used, not an effective Deff = D/τ. Real media have τ ≈ 1.5–3, so tdiff is a lower bound. Applied consistently in Ra and tdiff, so their ratio is unaffected.
  2. Rac = 4π² assumes the classical configuration (saturated layer, impermeable base, fixed-concentration top). Check it against your actual boundary conditions.
  3. Ideal molar-volume mixing and a concentration-independent V̅m — defensible at x ≈ 3×10−4.
  4. tconv is a lower bound on transit: it assumes a parcel at the full buoyancy velocity, the terminal speed of a developed finger. Early fingers are slower.
  5. Onset and transit are separate. For ROBU0 onset is negligible against transit; for ROBU2 the onset wait dominates entirely. Read both cards together.
  6. Equilibrium saturation. xsat is the equilibrium value; no interfacial mass-transfer kinetics are modelled.
  7. Homogeneous, isotropic, isothermal. No heterogeneity, no dispersion, no thermal coupling. Dissolution is exothermic (~19 kJ/mol), enough for a ~0.5 K transient when a sub-mL pore volume saturates.
  8. The bulk Xe phase is assumed held in place. Xe is dense: above ~6.2 MPa at 20 °C (8.4 MPa at 40 °C) the bulk Xe phase is denser than water and would sink if free to migrate, which would put the Xe below the water and make the arrangement gravitationally stable. A hydrophilic membrane retaining the phase removes this concern, and the dissolved-phase buoyancy driving the instability (§4) is unaffected either way. The dashboard marks the crossover for reference only.
  9. Continuum (Darcy) description assumed valid. This is the weakest assumption for the coarse grades. The finger scale ℓ* = φD/uD falls well below one pore diameter for ROBU00 (ℓ*/d ≈ 0.01), ROBU0 (0.05) and ROBU1 (0.18), so there is no valid REV and Ra in the thousands should not be read as a quantitative instability margin. Pore-scale Péclet numbers of 5–120 for the same grades mean mechanical dispersion rather than molecular D controls mixing, which would lower the effective Ra. ROBU2 (ℓ*/d ≈ 1.5, Pe ≈ 0.7) is the grade where the theory is self-consistent.
  10. Rac is a steady-layer criterion. Dissolution-driven convection is a transient boundary-layer problem; onset is governed by boundary-layer growth (the t* scaling of §7), not by the layer Ra. With Ra in the thousands the layer criterion is trivially satisfied and carries little information. Lateral confinement in a narrow core also raises the true threshold above 4π².
  11. Monochromatic beam assumed at the K-edge. The 5.4× edge jump requires sitting cleanly above 34.5614 keV; a bandwidth straddling the edge dilutes the contrast advantage substantially.

12. Xe saturation volume calculator

The saturation calculator estimates the volume of Xe gas (at tank pressure and temperature) required to reach 100% saturation of a given volume of water at target pore conditions.

Physical basis

At equilibrium, the dissolved Xe mole fraction in water is determined by Henry's law with Krichevsky–Kasarnovsky pressure correction:

xsat = f / KK(T,P)

KK(T,P) = H(T) exp( V̅m(P − Psat_w) / RT ) xsat mole fraction of dissolved Xe, f fugacity of Xe in the tank (Pa), H(T) Henry's constant (Pa·m³/mol), V̅m partial molar volume (46.5 cm³/mol), Psatw water vapour pressure.

Solver procedure

  1. Compute tank Xe fugacity: Use the residual-Helmholtz EoS (Lemmon & Span 2006) to find the fugacity coefficient φ(Ptank, Ttank), then f = φ × Ptank.
  2. Compute pore partition coefficient: Evaluate KK at the target pore conditions (Ppore, Tpore).
  3. Compute saturation mole fraction: xsat = f / KK.
  4. Compute dissolved Xe: From xsat and water amount, calculate moles Xe needed: nXe = xsat × nwater / (1 − xsat).
  5. Compute Xe volume at tank: Use the tank EoS to find the molar volume vm at (Ptank, Ttank), then V = nXe × vm.

Hydrate stability

The tool warns if either tank or pore conditions approach the Xe clathrate hydrate boundary (structure I, H-Lw-V equilibrium). The measured boundary spans 273–343 K and 0.15–376 MPa [Rasoolzadeh et al. 2020] and is interpolated via log-linear fit across 87 experimental points. Operating safely below this boundary requires elevated temperature (≥30–35°C) or moderate pressure limits; beamtime commonly runs at 40 °C to ensure a comfortable margin.
